# Fact-check: Charlie Munger dossier

Checked 2026-08-08. Method: adversarial verification of every doctrine entry, the Characteristic objection section, and the Blind spots section. Assumed wrong until proven otherwise.

Access note: on this machine the network egress proxy blocks *all* direct page fetches (stripe.press, jamesclear.com, fs.blog, speakola.com, biznews.com, novelinvestor.com, quartr.com, cnbc.com, en.wikipedia.org and every other domain tried returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`, and `curl` gets a 403 CONNECT tunnel failure). Verification was therefore done by (a) exact-phrase search-engine queries against the primary URLs, corroborated across multiple independent mirrors/transcripts, and (b) the GitHub code-search API against a public repository of full Berkshire Hathaway annual-meeting transcripts (`pzponge/Yestoday`), which returned verbatim transcript fragments for the 2000 and 2003 meetings — the two entries where the exact meeting year was the thing under test. Where a claim rests only on secondary sources, that is stated.

## Verdicts

### D1 — CORRECTED
Checked venue, date, Talk number, Carson's prescriptions, Munger's added prescriptions, and the Jacobi attribution.
- Venue and date confirmed: commencement address to the Harvard School, Los Angeles (the boys' prep school where Munger was a longtime trustee; now Harvard-Westlake), June 13, 1986. Confirmed across multiple independent mirrors of the transcript.
- Talk number confirmed: Poor Charlie's Almanack lists it as Talk One ("Harvard School Commencement Speech"); the Stripe Press edition's own path for it is `/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-one`.
- Carson's three prescriptions confirmed verbatim in substance: ingesting chemicals in an effort to alter mood or perception; envy; resentment.
- Munger's four added prescriptions confirmed and in the dossier's order: (1) "First, be unreliable. Do not faithfully do what you have engaged to do." (2) "learn everything you possibly can from your own personal experience, minimizing what you learn vicariously from the good and bad experience of others, living and dead." (3) "go down and stay down when you get your first, second, or third severe reverse in the battle of life." (4) ignore the rustic who said "I wish I knew where I was going to die, and then I'd never go there" — i.e. ignore inversion itself. The dossier's compressed renderings of all four are faithful.
- Jacobi confirmed: "The great algebraist, Jacobi, had exactly the same approach as Carson and was known for his constant repetition of one phrase: 'Invert, always invert.'" The dossier's quoted fragment matches, and its gloss ("many hard problems are best attacked backward") tracks Munger's "many hard problems are best solved only when they are addressed backward." Calling Jacobi "the algebraist Carl Jacobi" is Munger's own characterization (Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi was a mathematician; Munger's word is "algebraist"), so no defect.
- DEFECT (quote precision): the dossier puts `"prescriptions for guaranteed misery in life"` inside quotation marks as Munger's words. It is not verbatim. Munger's text is: Carson "couldn't tell the graduating class how to be happy, but he could tell them from personal experience how to guarantee misery," and "Carson's prescriptions for sure misery included..." The quoted string in the dossier matches secondary-source summaries (e.g. Quartr's write-up), not the speech.
- Exact replacement wording for the defective clause only: `instead of prescribing success, he passed on and extended Johnny Carson's account of "how to guarantee misery" — what Munger called "Carson's prescriptions for sure misery"`
- Everything else in the entry stands as written.

### D2 — CORRECTED
Checked the source line, the latticework quote, the model count, the multi-discipline claim, and the hammer/nail line.
- Latticework quote confirmed verbatim: "the first rule is that you can't really know anything if you just remember isolated facts and try and bang 'em back. If the facts don't hang together on a latticework of theory, you don't have them in a usable form."
- Model count confirmed: "80 or 90 important models will carry about 90% of the freight in making you a worldly-wise person." The dossier's hedge ("roughly 80 or 90, he estimated") is accurate.
- Multi-discipline argument confirmed ("the models have to come from multiple disciplines — because all the wisdom of the world is not to be found in one little academic department"), as is the man-with-a-hammer line ("To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks pretty much like a nail"). The disciplines the dossier lists (mathematics, accounting, engineering, statistics, psychology, microeconomics) are the sequence Munger actually works through in the talk.
- Talk number confirmed: Talk Two of Poor Charlie's Almanack (Stripe Press path `/poor-charlies-almanack/talk-two`); the talk is also widely circulated as "The Art of Stock Picking."
- DEFECT (anachronistic venue name): the dossier cites "USC Marshall School of Business, 1994." USC's business school was not called Marshall in 1994 — it was renamed the Marshall School of Business in 1997, after a $35M gift from alumnus Gordon Marshall. Poor Charlie's Almanack and every transcript cite the venue simply as "USC Business School." (A second, unrelated trap worth knowing: Speakola's page title says "USC Business School – 1995"; that reflects the May 1995 publication of the transcript in Outstanding Investor Digest, not the delivery date. 1994 is correct.)
- Exact replacement wording for the defective clause only: `USC Business School, 1994 (the school was not named for Gordon Marshall until a 1997 gift; Poor Charlie's Almanack cites it simply as "USC Business School")`
- Everything else in the entry stands as written.

### D3 — CONFIRMED
Checked the quote, the source, and both halves of the illustrative contrast.
- Quote confirmed essentially verbatim from the 1994 USC talk: "So you have to figure out what your own aptitudes are. If you play games where other people have the aptitudes and you don't, you are going to lose. And that is as close to certain as any prediction that you can make." The dossier's rendering ("you're going to lose. And that's as close to certain as any prediction that you can make") differs only in contractions across transcript variants. No defect.
- Bemidji contrast confirmed as being in this same 1994 talk (not a later one): becoming the best tennis player in the world is hopeless for almost everyone, whereas becoming "the best plumbing contractor in Bemidji" is "probably doable by two-thirds of you" with enough discipline. Correctly sourced.
- Attribution note (not a defect): the phrase "circle of competence" is Buffett's, formalized in his 1996 shareholder letter ("You only have to be able to evaluate companies within your circle of competence"); Munger's own framing in this talk is aptitudes and edge. The dossier uses "circle of competence" as a heading/label while sourcing the substance and the quote correctly to Munger, so nothing is misattributed — but the label itself is Buffett's coinage, worth knowing before quoting it as "Munger's term."
- Second note: Munger's Bemidji example arguably cuts slightly against the dossier's gloss that the circle "is hard to enlarge" — his point there is that the plumbing circle *is* enterable by two-thirds of the audience given discipline. The gloss is defensible as a synthesis of his aptitudes argument, so no correction issued.

### D4 — CONFIRMED
Checked venue/date, the Talk Eleven relationship, the top-5% line, both worked examples, the maxim, and the agency-cost antidote.
- Source confirmed: "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment," delivered at Harvard University in June 1995; revised by Munger in 2005 and published as Talk Eleven of Poor Charlie's Almanack (first edition 2005, Donning Company, ed. Peter D. Kaufman). Both halves of the dossier's citation are right.
- Nuance worth carrying (not a defect): Talk Eleven is not a straight revision of the 1995 speech alone. The book describes it as selections from three of Munger's talks combined into "one talk never made," with considerable new material added in the 2005 revision — which is also why the tendency count differs (24 causes in the 1995 speech, 25 tendencies in the revised version). The dossier's phrasing "revised by Munger as Talk Eleven" is accurate as far as it goes but slightly understates the recombination.
- Incentives do open the catalog in both versions (the 1995 speech starts with the underrecognition of reinforcement/incentives; the revised talk's tendency #1 is Reward and Punishment Superresponse Tendency). Confirmed.
- Top-5% line confirmed; Munger's precise wording is "I think I've been in the top five percent of my age cohort almost all my adult life in understanding the power of incentives, and yet I've always underestimated that power." The dossier says "all his life" where Munger says "almost all my adult life" — the dossier does not use quotation marks there, so this is acceptable paraphrase, but the precise wording is narrower.
- FedEx confirmed: the night-shift sorting problem was solved when FedEx paid the shift rather than the hour. Xerox confirmed: Joe Wilson returned to Xerox because a better new machine was selling worse than the older inferior one, the commission arrangement having given salesmen a perverse incentive.
- Maxim confirmed verbatim: "Never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives."
- Agency-cost antidote confirmed, and confirmed as Munger's own framing, not the dossier's inference: "incentive-caused bias, both in one's own mind and that of one's trusted advisor, creates what economists call agency costs... widespread incentive-caused bias requires that one should often distrust, or take with a grain of salt, the advice of one's professional advisor, even if he is an engineer." Munger's three stated antidotes follow (fear advice that is especially good for the advisor; learn the basic elements of the advisor's trade; double-check, disbelieve or replace much of what you're told).

### D5 — CONFIRMED
Checked the definition, the trigger conditions, the business example, the inaction/axe-murder passage, Serpico, and the closing quote.
- Definition confirmed: Social-Proof Tendency is "the automatic tendency of man to think and act as he sees others around him thinking and acting," and it "triggers most easily in the presence of puzzlement or stress, and particularly when both exist."
- Business example confirmed. Munger's canonical version is the fertilizer one: "one oil company bought a fertilizer company, and every other major oil company practically ran out and bought a fertilizer company... if Exxon was doing it, it was good enough for Mobil." The revised talk carries the mine/fertilizer pairing the dossier uses. Correct either way.
- Inaction and directors confirmed: "In the presence of doubt, inaction by others becomes social proof that inaction is the right course," and outside directors "usually display a near-ultimate form of inaction," failing to object "to anything much short of an axe murder" until public embarrassment forces intervention. The dossier's compression is faithful.
- Serpico confirmed as Munger's own coinage in this talk, named for Frank Serpico and the near-totally corrupt NYPD division he resisted; Munger's point is exactly the dossier's — social proof plus incentive-caused bias makes evil behavior spread until an organization is rotten.
- Closing quote — minor precision note only: Munger's line is "Learn how to ignore *these* examples from others when they are wrong, because few skills are more worth having." The dossier writes "the examples." One-word variance inside quotation marks; not material, no correction issued, but worth fixing if the line is ever quoted publicly.
- The "isolated, stressed, and surrounded by compliant others" gloss about disreputable sales operations is a fair synthesis of Munger's cult/sales material under this tendency; nothing overstated.

### D6 — CONFIRMED
Checked the source, the pari-mutuel framing, and both quotes.
- Correctly sourced to the 1994 USC talk's pari-mutuel passage.
- Framing confirmed: the racetrack pari-mutuel pool means everything obviously good about a horse is already reflected in the odds, so the only edge is the occasional mispriced bet.
- Quote confirmed verbatim: "The wise ones bet heavily when the world offers them that opportunity. They bet big when they have the odds. And the rest of the time, they don't. It's just that simple."
- Second quote confirmed: "...and yet, in investment management, practically nobody operates that way" (Munger's fuller sentence adds that he and Buffett do operate that way and are not alone, but that "a huge majority of people have some other crazy construct in their heads").
- The corollary drawn (few, heavily weighted, well-understood positions rather than constant activity) is the talk's own conclusion. No inflation.

### D7 — CORRECTED
This is the entry with the real sourcing defect. Checked the quote, the coinage, and the stated rationale — they come from two different places.
- The quoted sentence IS verbatim and IS from the 2000 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting (morning session, April 29, 2000). Transcript fragment retrieved via GitHub code search against a full-transcript repository: "The investing where you find a few great companies and just sit on your ass because you've correctly predicted the future, that is what it's very nice to be good at." Immediately preceded by Munger contrasting it with buying undervalued things you then have to sell as they approach intrinsic value ("it's an active kind of investing"), and immediately followed by Buffett's joke that "the movie was G-rated even though —". So the dossier's quote and its 2000 attribution are correct.
- DEFECT: everything else in the entry is sourced to the wrong talk. The *coinage* "sit-on-your-ass investing" and the *rationale the dossier attaches to it* — lower brokerage costs and the tax advantage of not realizing gains — are from Munger's 1994 USC talk (the same talk cited in D2/D3/D6, circulated as "The Art of Stock Picking" / "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom"), where he says: "There are huge advantages for an individual to get into a position where you make a few great investments and just sit on your ass: you are paying less to brokers, you are listening to less nonsense, and if it works, the governmental tax system gives you an extra one, two or three percentage points per annum compounded." At the 2000 meeting Munger used the words "sit on your ass" in passing; he did not there coin the label or give the broker/tax rationale. A full-text search of the annual-meeting transcript corpus returns exactly one "sit on your ass" hit — the 2000 sentence above — with no accompanying broker/tax argument.
- Exact replacement wording for the Source line: `**Source:** "A Lesson on Elementary, Worldly Wisdom" / "The Art of Stock Picking," USC Business School, 1994 (the "sit on your ass investing" coinage and its broker-cost/tax rationale); Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting, April 29, 2000 (the quoted remark)`
- Exact replacement wording for the defective opening clause of the body: `Munger coined "sit-on-your-ass investing" in the 1994 USC talk, and returned to the idea at the 2000 Berkshire meeting:`
- The doctrinal content of the entry is otherwise sound: the virtues Munger attaches to it are indeed practical rather than aesthetic ("you are paying less to brokers, you are listening to less nonsense," plus one to three points a year of deferred-tax compounding), and its pairing with D6 is a fair synthesis rather than a claim about a specific source.

### D8 — CONFIRMED
Checked the meeting year, the exchange, and the quote word by word. This is the entry most exposed to the "wrong meeting" trap, and it survives.
- Verbatim transcript fragment retrieved from the 2003 Berkshire Hathaway annual meeting: "CHARLIE MUNGER: Never. And this is a very interesting thing that's happened. If you take the most powerful freshman text in economics, which is by [Greg] Mankiw of Harvard, and he says on practically the first page that 'intelligent people make their decisions based on opportunity cost.' In other words, it's your alternatives that are competing for the use of your time or money, that matter in judging whether you take action or not."
- The "Never" is Munger answering Buffett, who had just said Berkshire doesn't know its cost of capital, has never seen a cost-of-capital calculation that made sense, and measures everything against alternatives — so the dossier's setup ("Asked about cost of capital at the 2003 meeting, Munger dismissed academic formulas") is accurate.
- The dossier's ellipsis (`which is by Mankiw of Harvard, ... he says on practically the first page`) elides only the connecting "and" and the bracketed first name. Faithful.
- The Berkshire-practice gloss (measure any new commitment against the best opportunity already on the table) is Buffett and Munger's stated practice at that same meeting, not an embellishment.

### Characteristic objection — CONFIRMED
- The only direct quotation in the section, "never, ever, think about something else when you should be thinking about the power of incentives," is verbatim (verified under D4).
- The five-step structure is a synthesis of D1/D3/D4/D5/D8 rather than a claim about any single source, and each step maps to a doctrine that verified. The incentives-first ordering is faithful to Munger, who puts incentives at the head of the misjudgment catalog and repeatedly applies it to advisors specifically.
- The Bio Green–specific applications (Korean supplement science, Singapore consumer regulation, paid-acquisition economics) are the dossier author's extrapolation, clearly marked as such by context. Nothing is put in Munger's mouth. No defect.

### Blind spots — CORRECTED
Four of the five hold; one contains a defective clause.
- (1) Patience-doctrine non-transfer: fair. Munger never ran a pre-revenue consumer product startup. He did found a law firm (Munger, Tolles) and an investment partnership (Wheeler, Munger & Co.), and did Pasadena real-estate development from scratch — so "he never did" is true only in the specific sense the sentence uses (pre-revenue *consumer* startup). Accurate as written.
- (2) Contempt for hype-driven and fad categories: well-supported by his public record. Partial counterexample worth knowing: Munger championed Berkshire's BYD stake, a capital-intensive, fast-changing EV/battery business he called a miracle — so "avoided businesses he could not project decades out" is a strong generalization with at least one famous exception. Not a misstatement of his views; no correction issued.
- (3) DEFECT. The clause "his marketing views come from franchise businesses (Coca-Cola, See's) with decades of accumulated brand equity, not from creating demand from zero" is wrong on its second half. Munger's Talk Six in Poor Charlie's Almanack — "Practical Thought About Practical Thought?", delivered July 20, 1996 — is *entirely* an exercise in creating demand from zero: the Glotz's Cola problem, in which you must turn $2 million in 1884 Atlanta into a $2 trillion beverage business, and which Munger solves with Pavlovian conditioning, operant conditioning, social proof and psychological "lollapalooza" effects applied to a brand that does not yet exist. The rest of the blind spot (no doctrine on paid social, influencer marketing, or DTC funnel economics) is correct — Talk Six is about mass advertising and conditioning, not acquisition math — but the flat claim that he never theorized demand creation from zero is false.
- Exact replacement wording for the defective clause only: `his marketing views come from franchise businesses (Coca-Cola, See's) with decades of accumulated brand equity, and where he did theorize demand creation from zero — the Glotz's Cola exercise in "Practical Thought About Practical Thought?" (1996) — the tools are Pavlovian conditioning and social proof at mass-advertising scale, not acquisition math`
- (4) Base-rate pessimism calibrated for a rejecter rather than a committed founder: an analytical judgment, fairly stated, no factual claim to falsify.
- (5) Balance-sheet slack: accurate for Berkshire-era Munger. Context that strengthens rather than undermines it: his own Wheeler, Munger & Co. partnership fell roughly 32% in 1973 and again in 1974 on a concentrated book and he wound it up in 1975 — evidence that concentration without slack hurt even Munger.

### Voice — CONFIRMED
- No falsifiable factual claims beyond the list of originators he credits, and that list checks out: Jacobi (D1), Ben Graham, Robert Cialdini (Munger repeatedly credits Influence and famously sent Cialdini Berkshire stock), and Benjamin Franklin (the Poor Richard conceit that names the book).
- "I have nothing to add" is his documented catchphrase at Berkshire annual meetings. The stylistic description is consistent with the transcripts read for this check.

## Summary
- Confirmed: 5 (D3, D4, D5, D6, D8)
- Corrected: 3 (D1 — non-verbatim quoted phrase; D2 — "USC Marshall School of Business" is anachronistic for 1994; D7 — the "sit-on-your-ass investing" coinage and its rationale are from the 1994 USC talk, not the 2000 meeting)
- Rejected: 0
- Sections: Characteristic objection CONFIRMED; Blind spots CORRECTED (one clause — Munger did theorize demand creation from zero, in the 1996 Glotz's Cola talk); Voice CONFIRMED

Overall reliability: high, with one real sourcing error. Given how much fake Munger material circulates, the striking result is that every direct quotation in this dossier is genuine and traceable — the Jacobi "Invert, always invert" line, the latticework sentence, the "80 or 90 models" estimate, the aptitudes/"as close to certain" line, the pari-mutuel "bet big when they have the odds" passage, the "never, ever, think about something else" maxim, the social-proof and axe-murder passages, the 2000 "sit on your ass" sentence, and the 2003 Mankiw sentence all check out against transcripts, two of them (2000 and 2003) against verbatim annual-meeting transcript text. No fabricated quote and no Buffett line misattributed to Munger was found. The two traps the dossier walks past cleanly are the ones most people fail: it dates the 1994 USC talk to 1994 rather than the 1995 Outstanding Investor Digest publication year, and it puts the Mankiw opportunity-cost remark at the 2003 meeting, which is exactly right. The one substantive defect is D7: the dossier stages the whole entry at the 2000 Berkshire meeting when only the quoted sentence is from there — the coinage itself and the broker-cost/tax-deferral rationale are from the 1994 USC talk, six years earlier. Two smaller repairs: the misery-speech phrase in D1 is a secondary-source paraphrase presented as a quotation, and "USC Marshall School of Business" in D2 post-dates the talk by three years. Two context notes to carry: Talk Eleven is a 2005 recombination of three talks rather than a straight revision of the June 1995 Harvard speech (24 causes then, 25 tendencies after), and "circle of competence" — the D3 heading — is Buffett's phrase from his 1996 letter, not Munger's, even though the argument and the quote under it are genuinely Munger's.
