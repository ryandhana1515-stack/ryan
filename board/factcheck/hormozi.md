# Fact-check: Alex Hormozi dossier

Checked 2026-08-08. Method: adversarial verification of every doctrine entry against primary sources where reachable. Most book-text and summary hosts (shop.acquisition.com, goodreads.com, shortform.com, ebin.pub, deciphr.ai, gregfaxon.com, manassaloi.com, insta.page, substack mirrors and the circulating PDF on cdn.prod.website-files.com) are blocked by the network egress proxy — direct `curl` to them returns a 403 CONNECT rejection — so those were verified via search-engine snippets of the primary URLs plus multiple independent mirrors, published tables of contents, audiobook chapter listings, and quote databases. Assumed wrong until proven otherwise.

## Verdicts

### D1 — CONFIRMED
Checked the book/year, both chapter citations, the definition's four components, and the worked comparison.
- $100M Offers, published 17 July 2021 (Acquisition.com $100M Series). Correct.
- Section structure verified against multiple independent tables of contents: Section I "How We Got Here" contains ch. 1 "How We Got Here" and ch. 2 "Grand Slam Offers"; Section II "Pricing" contains ch. 3 "Pricing: The Commodity Problem", ch. 4 "Pricing: Finding the Right Market — A Starving Crowd", ch. 5 "Pricing: Charge What It's Worth". Both citations in the Source line are exact, and the "commoditization argument continued in 'Pricing: The Commodity Problem'" relationship is right.
- Definition confirmed: "an offer you present to the marketplace that cannot be compared to any other product or service available, combining an attractive promotion, an unmatchable value proposition, a premium price, and an unbeatable guarantee." The dossier's four components are verbatim-accurate — including the two most commonly mangled ones ("attractive promotion," not "attractive promise"; "unmatchable value proposition," not "unbeatable value"). Context note, not a defect: Hormozi's full definition adds a fifth clause — "with a money model (payment terms) that allows you to get paid to get new customers" — which is the seed of D8.
- The worked comparison is real: the same $10,000 ad spend produces roughly $5,000 collected on a commoditized agency offer versus $112,000 on the Grand Slam Offer version. The dossier's vaguer phrasing ("radically different economics") understates rather than inflates — the correct direction for a fact-check.
- "Fix the offer first" as the explicit ordering is correct: $100M Leads is written to sit on top of $100M Offers and states that it assumes a Grand Slam Offer already exists.

### D2 — CONFIRMED
Checked the chapter citation, the formula, all four drivers, and the infinity claim.
- Chapter confirmed: "Value Offer: The Value Equation," ch. 6, Section III ("Value — Create Your Offer"). Exact match.
- Formula confirmed: Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood of Achievement) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice). All four drivers named correctly, including the easily-garbled "Perceived Likelihood of Achievement" (retellings routinely shorten this to "likelihood of success") and "Effort & Sacrifice" (often mangled into "effort and cost," which would wrongly put price inside the equation).
- The infinity claim is the book's own, not a retelling artifact: because the equation divides, driving time delay and effort/sacrifice toward zero drives value toward infinity ("if you can reduce your prospects' true time delay to receiving value to zero... and your effort and sacrifice is zero, you have an infinitely valuable product").
- "Increase the top two, decrease the bottom two; the best Grand Slam Offers do all four at once" is the book's stated method, and the dossier's emphasis on the denominator as the less-contested lever matches Hormozi's own argument that every competitor already advertises the dream outcome.
- Attribution note (not a defect): the dossier claims only that this is "his single most-cited framework," never that he invented it. Worth knowing that the shape — a product of desirables over a product of undesirables — is the same as Maister/Green/Galford's Trust Equation (The Trusted Advisor, 2000), which Hormozi does not cite. Since the dossier asserts no originality, there is nothing to correct.

### D3 — CONFIRMED
Checked the chapter citation, the virtuous/vicious cycle, and the Dan Kennedy attribution (the key trap in this entry).
- "Pricing: Charge What It's Worth" is ch. 5, Section II. Exact match.
- The virtuous cycle / vicious cycle of price is genuinely the book's argument, in both directions as the dossier states: higher price → greater client emotional investment and better client results → more cash to reinvest in delivery and marketing → higher-caliber clients; cutting price → thinner margin → worse service → worse reviews → forced further discounting.
- The Dan Kennedy line is real and correctly attributed. Hormozi quotes Kennedy inside $100M Offers: "There is no strategic benefit to being the second cheapest in the marketplace, but there is for being the most expensive." The dossier's instruction to keep the Kennedy attribution when citing it is exactly right, and is the best piece of attribution hygiene in the file — this is the borrowed-idea trap most Hormozi retellings fail.
- "Charge so much it feels almost uncomfortable" is a fair unquoted paraphrase of his actual formulations ("charge as much money for your products or services as humanly possible" and "charge as high a price as you can say out loud without cracking a smile"). No quotation marks are used, so this is acceptable.

### D4 — CONFIRMED
Checked the source line, the two axes, the four channels, and the sequencing.
- $100M Leads, published 19 August 2023. Correct. Section III is titled "Get Leads" and contains exactly the four chapters cited: "#1 Warm Outreach," "#2 Post Free Content" (Parts I and II), "#3 Cold Outreach," "#4 Run Paid Ads" (Parts I and II). The Source line matches the book's own structure and naming.
- The two axes are correct as stated: warm (people who know you) vs. cold (strangers), and private/one-to-one vs. public/one-to-many. Warm+private = warm outreach; warm+public = post free content; cold+private = cold outreach; cold+public = paid ads. This is Hormozi's own 2×2, not a summarizer's reconstruction.
- "Every business in history has only ever used these four ways to advertise" is his actual claim, and the anti-overwhelm diagnosis (most operators run one channel at partial capacity while believing the market is exhausted) is his.
- Sequencing — warm outreach first, then content, then cold outreach and paid ads as cash and proof accumulate — matches the Roadmap chapter in Section V. Confirmed.

### D5 — CONFIRMED (one citation-precision note)
Checked the chapter citation, the lead-magnet rules, and the provenance of the signature line.
- Section is right (Section II, "Get Understanding"), but the chapter's actual title is "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets," not "Lead Magnets." Loose rather than wrong; if precision matters, the Source line should read: `$100M Leads (2023), "Engage Your Leads: Offers and Lead Magnets" chapter (Section II)`.
- The lead-magnet doctrine checks out in detail. Hormozi's three routes are: solve one narrow problem completely; reveal a problem the prospect didn't know they had; or deliver one step of a multi-step process perfectly. His quality bar is that the free thing must be good enough that the prospect assumes the paid thing is incredible, testable by asking whether the audience would normally have paid for it. The problem-solution cycle (solving one problem reveals the next — illustrated by free pretzels creating thirst) is exactly the mechanism the dossier describes, and the dossier's "solve a narrow, real problem completely" is his wording, not a softening.
- "Give away the secrets, sell the implementation" is a genuine Hormozi line — used in $100M Leads and posted verbatim by him on X — with his stated rationale that only a small fraction of people will ever implement, so the rest buy the implementation. Correctly framed as his.
- Honest-framing note: the underlying "give value first" posture is not original to Hormozi (Godin, Vaynerchuk and the whole direct-response lead-magnet tradition precede it). The dossier claims only that it is "his stated rule," which is accurate.

### D6 — CONFIRMED (one chapter-title precision note, one over-generalization note)
Checked the metric, the ratio, the chapter, and the asymmetric diagnosis.
- LTGP (lifetime gross profit = lifetime customer revenue minus cost of delivery), used deliberately in place of revenue-based LTV, is his actual metric and his actual reasoning — gross profit is the money actually available to buy customers. Confirmed.
- The 3:1 floor is his stated benchmark: businesses below 3:1 struggle to scale, and getting above 3:1 is his readiness gate before pouring money into ads. Confirmed across multiple independent renderings of the paid-ads material.
- Chapter-title precision: the book's chapter is "#4 Run Paid Ads Part II: Money Stuff." The dossier writes "Paid Ads Part II — The Money Stuff," inserting a definite article. Trivial, but it is not the exact title.
- The asymmetric diagnosis is genuinely his and is the strongest part of the entry: he argues raising LTGP beats lowering CAC because LTGP can grow without limit while CAC can only fall to zero — i.e. a bad ratio is usually a monetization/offer problem, not a traffic problem. Confirmed.
- Over-generalization note (adversarial, not a factual error): 3:1 is his floor for reasonably automated, high-gross-margin businesses. Elsewhere in the same body of work he states much higher requirements for labor-heavy models (manual-outreach businesses needing a minimum around 20:1 and ideally 30:1) and says a heavily scaled automated business can survive nearer 1.5:1. The dossier's "roughly 3:1" is hedged enough to stand, but the number is not one-size-fits-all — and BIO N:OV, with physical fulfillment, sits nearer the labor/COGS-heavy end than the software end.

### D7 — CORRECTED
Checked the chapter title, the Rule of 100's content, the More-Better-New ordering, and the section number. One citation error.
- Chapter title is exact: "Core Four On Steroids: More Better New" is a real chapter, and it does carry both the More-Better-New ordering and the Rule of 100 commitment.
- DEFECT: the dossier places it in **Section IV**. It is the closing chapter of **Section III ("Get Leads")**. Section IV of $100M Leads is "Get Lead Getters" (customer referrals, employees, agencies, affiliates and partners) — a different subject entirely, and the one Ryan would actually want for an affiliate programme. Verified against two independent full tables of contents and against the audiobook chapter sequence (Part 7 = Core Four on Steroids, i.e. before the lead-getter chapters).
- Exact replacement wording for the Source line: `**Source:** $100M Leads (2023), Section III "Get Leads", closing chapter "Core Four On Steroids: More Better New" (Rule of 100)`.
- Everything else in the entry stands. The Rule of 100 content is accurate: "100 primary actions every day, for one hundred days in a row," with the book's four variants being 100 warm reach-outs/day, 100 minutes of content/day, 100 cold reach-outs/day, and $100/day in ads — all three of the dossier's examples are the book's own. More-Better-New is correctly ordered and correctly diagnosed (do far more of what works, then improve it, then add new; jumping to "new" first is his named common error).
- Minor precision note: the book's rule is 100 actions/day *for 100 days in a row*, then reassess; the dossier's "per day, every day... sustained without missing days" drops the 100-day frame and reads as open-ended. The dossier's own Characteristic-objection section restores the "for 100 days" figure, so the file is internally consistent.

### D8 — CONFIRMED
Checked the book/year, the stage structure, the 30-day condition, and the "client-financed acquisition" provenance.
- $100M Money Models published 16 August 2025 (Acquisition.com $100M Series). Year correct.
- Stage structure confirmed: Stage I "Get Cash" (attraction offers), Stage II "Get More Cash" (upsells and downsells), Stage III "Get the Most Cash" (continuity). The dossier's "attraction offers → immediate upsells/downsells → continuity" sequence and its "Stage I 'Get Cash' attraction offers through continuity" citation both match the book.
- The 30-day/two-customers claim is accurate and, notably, is the precise version rather than the inflated retelling: the book's condition is gross profit collected in the first 30 days exceeding 2 × (CAC + COGS) — i.e. enough cash from one customer inside 30 days to acquire *and fulfil* two more. The dossier says "get and service at least two more," which correctly includes the fulfilment term that most summaries drop.
- "Client-financed acquisition" as his earlier Gym Launch-era framing is correct; CFA is his term and predates the 2025 book.
- Attribution note (not a defect): the underlying mechanic — break even or better on the front end so the back end is free growth — is classic direct-response doctrine (the self-liquidating offer; Kennedy/Abraham-era front-end/back-end economics). The dossier does not claim Hormozi invented it, only that he named and formalised it, which is defensible.

### Characteristic objection — CONFIRMED (two precision notes)
- The stance is accurate. "You don't have a traffic problem, you have an offer problem" is a documented Hormozi position and the explicit thesis ordering of $100M Offers → $100M Leads. The diagnostic questions attributed to him (what is the offer, why can't it be price-compared, gross margin per unit, LTGP, CAC, the ratio, 30-day cash vs. cost to acquire) are all metrics he actually uses, drawn from D2/D3/D6/D8 material verified above.
- Quote-precision note: the sentence in quotation marks — "have you done 100 warm outreaches a day for 100 days? No? Then you don't have a leads problem, you have a work problem" — is a constructed illustration, not a documented Hormozi quotation. It is faithful in substance (it correctly combines the Rule of 100 with his offer-problem retort), but it should not be repeated as a citation. His documented equivalents are "You don't have a sales problem or a marketing problem. You have an offer problem" and "Nobody knows you exist. Advertise more."
- Nuance note: "reliably refuses to discuss traffic tactics" is slightly stronger than the record. Hormozi wrote a full book of traffic tactics and his most-repeated public line is that most operators simply have not advertised enough. The dossier's qualifier — "until the offer and the unit economics are proven" — keeps it accurate, but do not expect him to be anti-advertising; he is anti-advertising-a-broken-offer.
- The pre-revenue prescription (sell manually one-to-one via warm outreach, prove people pay a premium, only then buy strangers' attention) matches his Roadmap chapter exactly. Confirmed.

### Blind spots — CORRECTED
The analytical judgments (service-margin doctrine transferring poorly to a physical supplement, CFA math at sub-$100 AOV, guarantees on ingestibles, no LTGP data pre-revenue, US-market benchmarks) are fair characterisations, not misstatements of Hormozi's views, and the regulatory framing is broadly right. One factual claim is out of date.
- Singapore HSA: confirmed. Health-supplement claims may not refer directly or indirectly to the 19 diseases and conditions listed in the Medicines Act; claims must not be false or misleading; supplements may describe support or maintenance of normal body function and only for healthy populations. HSA also updated its prohibited/restricted-ingredient guidelines on 29 October 2025 — relevant to a nitric-oxide formulation and worth checking against the actual ingredient list.
- DEFECT: "Meta/TikTok ad policies that reject or throttle before-after and disease/performance claims" is stale as of the date of this check. Meta moved its Health & Wellness Advertising Standards from product-based to claims-based enforcement effective 22 July 2026: before/after and side-by-side imagery for weight-loss and cosmetic products is **no longer auto-rejected**, and supplements lost blanket 18+ targeting restrictions. The ad is violative only when the imagery is paired with prohibited claims (misleading efficacy promises, statements implying inferiority of the viewer's body). TikTok's supplement/medical restrictions remain tighter and are enforced less consistently.
- Exact replacement wording for the defective clause: "and Meta/TikTok ad policies that restrict health claims — noting that since Meta's 22 July 2026 shift to claims-based enforcement the copy, not the imagery, is the compliance battleground: before/after and side-by-side images are no longer auto-rejected, while disease and efficacy claims still are, and TikTok remains stricter and less predictable."
- The practical conclusion the dossier draws from that clause — work the value-equation levers through compliant framing (mechanism education, third-party testing, sensory/experience outcomes) rather than copying his gym ads — survives the correction unchanged, and is if anything reinforced: under claims-based enforcement, the copy is what gets you rejected.

### Voice — CONFIRMED
No checkable factual claims. The stylistic description (formula-first, quantifies before opining, allergic to excuses and to novelty-as-procrastination, plain declaratives) is consistent with both books and his public interviews. No objection.

## Summary
- Confirmed: 7 (D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, D6, D8)
- Corrected: 1 (D7 — "Section IV" should be Section III; "Core Four On Steroids: More Better New" is the closing chapter of Section III "Get Leads", not part of Section IV "Get Lead Getters")
- Rejected: 0
- Non-doctrine sections: Characteristic objection CONFIRMED (with a note that its bracketed retort is a constructed illustration, not a real quotation); Blind spots CORRECTED (Meta's before/after policy changed on 22 July 2026); Voice CONFIRMED.

Overall reliability: high. This dossier survives adversarial checking better than the genre norm, and it specifically avoids the four traps that ruin most Hormozi write-ups. First, terminology: "Grand Slam Offer," the Value Equation and all four of its drivers, the Core Four, the Rule of 100, More-Better-New, LTGP:CAC and the money-model stages are all rendered in Hormozi's own words rather than the corrupted versions in circulation — including "Perceived Likelihood of Achievement" and "Effort & Sacrifice," which retellings usually flatten. Second, numbers: the dossier cites no Gym Launch or Acquisition.com revenue, portfolio or client-count figures at all, so none of the inflated numbers that attach to his name in secondary retellings made it in; the numeric claims it does make (3:1 LTGP:CAC, 30-day gross profit greater than twice CAC plus COGS, the $10k-ad-spend offer comparison) all check out, and the 30-day one is stated in the precise "acquire *and fulfil* two more" form that most summaries garble. Third, citations: there are no podcast or YouTube references to verify — every source is a book with a verifiable publication date (2021, 2023, 2025, all correct) and, in seven of eight entries, a chapter title that matches the published table of contents exactly. Fourth, borrowed ideas: the one place Hormozi is quoting someone else on a signature line — Dan Kennedy on never being the second cheapest — is explicitly credited, with an instruction to keep the credit. Three residual items worth carrying: the D7 section number is wrong (Section III, not IV); the quoted retort in the Characteristic objection is a constructed illustration and should not be circulated as a Hormozi quotation; and the Meta before/after policy claim in Blind spots was overtaken by Meta's 22 July 2026 move to claims-based enforcement, which shifts BIO N:OV's compliance exposure from imagery to copy.
