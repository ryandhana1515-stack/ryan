# Fact-check: Seth Godin dossier

Checked 2026-08-08. Method: adversarial verification of every doctrine entry against primary sources where reachable (seths.blog and ted.com are blocked by the network egress proxy, so those were verified via search-engine snippets of the primary URLs plus multiple independent mirrors/transcripts/summaries). Assumed wrong until proven otherwise.

## Verdicts

### D1 — CONFIRMED
Checked the definition quote, the source chain, the revocability claim, the behavioral test, and the interruption contrast.
- The exact quote "the privilege (not the right) of delivering anticipated, personal and relevant messages to people who actually want to get them" is verbatim from the blog post "Permission Marketing," seths.blog/2008/01/permission-mark/ — post exists, title and January 2008 date confirmed. The concept and "anticipated, personal, relevant" triad originate in Permission Marketing (1999), so "restated in his blog post" is the correct relationship.
- The behavioral test is real and in that same 2008 post: "Real permission works like this: if you stop showing up, people complain, they ask where you went" (illustrated with the Daily Candy anecdote), explicitly contrasted with legalistic/presumed permission (fine-print, no-complaints). The dossier's paraphrase "would the audience notice and complain" is faithful.
- Permission-as-asset vs. attention-rented-by-interruption, and interruption marketing losing effectiveness as clutter grows, are the book's core argument per multiple independent summaries.
- Attribution is honest: permission marketing is genuinely Godin's coinage (1999). No walk-back found — he reaffirmed it in "Ten years of Permission Marketing" (2009).

### D2 — CONFIRMED
Checked title, year, and each of the three claims.
- Purple Cow: Transform Your Business by Being Remarkable, first published May 2003 (Portfolio; revised edition 2009). Correct.
- "Remarkable = worth making a remark about" is Godin's own gloss; "the opposite of remarkable is very good" is a signature line of the book. Confirmed via Penguin Random House product page, Wikipedia, and multiple summaries.
- Marketing designed into the product rather than appended afterward: confirmed ("It's not a marketing function that you can slap on to your product... It's built right in, or it's not there").
- The inverted risk logic ("safe is risky") is the book's explicit argument. Confirmed. Framing as general doctrine is fair — this is the thesis of the whole book, not a stray remark.

### D3 — CONFIRMED
Checked talk existence, date, sliced-bread story, otaku, choices/time claim, edges-vs-middle prescription, and the attribution parenthetical.
- Talk exists: "How to get your ideas to spread," delivered at TED, February 2003 (Monterey); TED's own listing and the Internet Archive copy ("SethGodin_2003") confirm the year.
- Sliced bread: transcript-derived quote confirmed — "For the first 15 years after sliced bread was available no one bought it... until Wonder came along and figured out how to spread the idea of sliced bread, no one wanted it." The "roughly fifteen years" and Wonder Bread details are accurate.
- "Too many choices, too little time, ignore the ordinary stuff" is TED's own summary of the talk and appears in the transcript. Confirmed.
- Otaku is in the talk (hot-sauce otaku vs. no mustard otaku), as is the edges-vs-middle prescription ("smooth out the edges, go for the center... they would ignore the geeks, and God forbid, the laggards" — Godin argues the opposite: market to the people who care).
- Attribution check (the adversarial point): the diffusion curve with innovators/early adopters/laggards is Everett Rogers' framework (with Moore's chasm as the popularizer); the dossier already flags this ("the curve is borrowed; the 'market to the otaku-driven edges' prescription is his"). That is the correct crediting, and the talk itself uses the borrowed curve language. No defect.

### D4 — CONFIRMED
Checked both chapter citations and the content.
- This Is Marketing (2018) chapter titles verified against multiple independent tables of contents: ch. 4 "The Smallest Viable Market"; ch. 8 "More of the Who: Seeking the Smallest Viable Market"; ch. 9 "People Like Us Do Things Like This". All three match exactly.
- The ordering question ("What's the minimum number of people you would need to influence to make it worth the effort?"), "everyone" as a path to average, "they would miss you if you were gone," and growth outward via the stories the nucleus tells — all are the documented content of chs. 4/8. The ch. 9 identity/belonging link ("People like us do things like this") is correctly summarized.
- "Smallest viable market"/"minimum viable audience" is genuinely Godin's coinage (deliberately riffing on Eric Ries' "minimum viable product," which the dossier doesn't misattribute). Honest framing.

### D5 — CONFIRMED
Checked the quote and the two-questions formulation.
- "Marketing is the generous act of helping someone solve a problem. Their problem." — verbatim from This Is Marketing (widely quoted with page citation; Goodreads/quote databases and multiple book-note sites agree). The dossier's em-dash rendering ("— their problem") is a fair transcription of the two-sentence original.
- "Who's it for? What's it for?" is confirmed as his recurring formulation in the book and on the blog. The definition and framing appear in the opening chapters as cited (chs. 1–3). "Making change happen" as the definition of marketing ("Marketing is the generous act of helping others become who they seek to become") is the book's stated thesis. No inflation detected.

### D6 — CONFIRMED
Checked all three chapter citations and the content claims.
- Chapter titles verified: ch. 10 "Trust and Tension Create Forward Motion"; ch. 17 "Permission and Remarkability in a Virtuous Cycle"; ch. 18 "Trust Is as Scarce as Attention". All exact matches.
- Trust as the scarce resource in a low-trust world, earned through consistent showing up and kept promises, is the documented content of ch. 18. The permission→remarkability→trust virtuous cycle is ch. 17's titular argument. Tension-not-fear as the mechanism of forward motion (tension of "you might miss out on becoming who you want to be," distinct from fear) matches ch. 10 summaries. Faithful synthesis, correctly sourced.

### D7 — CORRECTED
Checked title, year, and content. One factual error in the framing.
- Book confirmed: The Dip: A Little Book That Teaches You When to Quit (and When to Stick), 2007. "Strategic quitting is the secret of successful organizations," "quit fast, quit often, quit without guilt," Dips create scarcity and scarcity creates value, and best-in-the-world with "world" and "best" defined by the customer's niche — all confirmed as the book's actual content.
- DEFECT: the dossier says "The Dip distinguishes two curves." The book distinguishes THREE curves: the Dip, the Cul-de-Sac (dead end), and the Cliff (where things seem fine until you fall off and the whole thing collapses — Godin treats it as rare, but it is a named third curve). Multiple independent summaries and the book excerpt confirm the three-curve structure.
- Exact replacement wording for the first clause: "The Dip distinguishes three curves: the Dip — the long slog between starting and mastery, which is hard precisely because it screens out the uncommitted and creates scarcity for those who push through — the Cul-de-Sac (dead end), where more effort never changes the outcome, and the rarer Cliff, which feels fine until it collapses all at once."
- Everything else in the entry stands as written.

### D8 — CONFIRMED
Checked book content, the blog post's existence, exact date, exact quote, and the Tribes claim.
- All Marketers Are Liars (2005): worldview-fitting stories, the consumer as the real "liar" (lying to themselves — "we tell ourselves stories that can't possibly be true because believing those stories allows us to function"), authenticity as the gate, and fib-vs-fraud collapse — all confirmed as the book's actual argument. "Is it true?" is verbatim one of the book's three gating questions ("What's your story? Will the people who need to hear this story believe it? Is it true?").
- Blog post confirmed: "Belief," seths.blog/2006/07/belief/ — July 2006, exactly as cited. The four-sentence quote is verbatim: "People don't believe what you tell them. They rarely believe what you show them. They often believe what their friends tell them. They always believe what they tell themselves." The claim that the line was later used in Tribes (2008) is corroborated — the quote is standardly attributed to Tribes in quote databases.
- Walk-back note (context, not a defect): Godin publicly retracted the TITLE — later printings cross out "Liars" and rebrand the book "All Marketers Tell Stories," with Godin saying "All marketers are storytellers. Only the losers are liars." The dossier's description of the doctrine already matches the post-walk-back position (authentic stories, consumer-as-liar), so the entry is not misleading, but anyone quoting the title should know he disowned it.

### Characteristic objection — CONFIRMED (one quote-precision note)
- Ch. 1 "Not Mass, Not Spam, Not Shameful" and ch. 16 "Price Is a Story" — both chapter titles verified exactly.
- "Low price as the last refuge of a marketer out of ideas" is a close paraphrase; Godin's actual line is "Low price is the last refuge of a marketer who has run out of generous ideas" (This Is Marketing; also delivered publicly as "...the marketer with nothing left to say"). The dossier does not use quotation marks, so this is acceptable paraphrase, but "generous ideas" is his precise wording.
- The opening questions ("Who's it for? What's it for? What change are you trying to make? Would anyone miss you?") are all documented Godin formulations (This Is Marketing; the "would they miss you" test traces to Permission Marketing doctrine, confirmed under D1). Faithful.

### Blind spots — CONFIRMED
Factual claims check out:
- Godin's blog dates to 2002 and he was already famous when it started (Permission Marketing was a 1999 bestseller; he had sold Yoyodyne to Yahoo! in 1998 and served as a Yahoo VP of direct marketing) — so "built his blog audience over two decades from an already-famous platform" is accurate, arguably understated.
- Health-supplement claims are indeed regulated in Singapore (HSA regulates health supplements and prohibits certain disease claims) and restricted by TikTok/Meta ad policies. Accurate.
- The characterization that Godin's doctrine contains no CAC/LTV math or media-buying guidance is fair; This Is Marketing explicitly disdains the attention-buying funnel rather than teaching it. These are honest analytical judgments, not misstatements of his views.

### Voice — CONFIRMED
No checkable factual claims; the stylistic description (aphoristic, question-led, short declaratives, second person, reframing over prescribing) is consistent with his blog and published interviews. No objection.

## Summary
- Confirmed: 7 (D1, D2, D3, D4, D5, D6, D8)
- Corrected: 1 (D7 — "two curves" should be three: Dip, Cul-de-Sac, Cliff)
- Rejected: 0

Overall reliability: high. This dossier is unusually careful for its genre. Every cited source exists as described — the January 2008 "Permission Marketing" post, the July 2006 "Belief" post, the February 2003 TED talk, and all eight This Is Marketing chapter-title citations (chs. 1, 4, 8, 9, 10, 16, 17, 18) verified exactly against the book's table of contents. All direct quotations checked out verbatim, including the four-sentence "Belief" quote and the "generous act" definition. The attribution hygiene is notably good: the one place where Godin builds on someone else's framework (the Rogers/Moore diffusion curve in the TED talk) is already flagged as borrowed in the dossier itself. The single factual error found — collapsing The Dip's three curves into two by omitting the Cliff — is minor and does not change the doctrine drawn from it. Two context notes worth carrying: Godin later disowned the All Marketers Are Liars title (reissued as All Marketers Tell Stories), and the "last refuge" price line is a paraphrase of "run out of generous ideas." Nothing in the dossier presents a narrow remark as general doctrine; the entries summarize book-length theses, not cherry-picked asides.
