## 3. Company deep-dive: TSMC (TSM) — deep-dive #2

**What it does.** TSMC is the world's dominant contract chipmaker — it
manufactures the chips that Nvidia, AMD, Apple, and Broadcom *design* but
cannot build themselves. It also does the advanced packaging (CoWoS) that
AI accelerators require.

**How it makes money.** It charges per wafer manufactured, with
leading-edge nodes (3nm, now ramping 2nm) commanding premium prices. The
High-Performance Computing segment — anchored by AI accelerators — is now
**66% of total wafer revenue**, up 20% quarter-on-quarter
([Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/company-news/tsmc-q2-2026-slides-ai-demand-drives-record-margins-hpc-surges-20-93CH-4794789)).

**Revenue trend.** Steeply up, five straight record quarters. Q2 2026:
revenue of NT$1,270.38B (US$40.2B), +36% year-on-year; management *raised*
full-year 2026 growth guidance to above 40% and lifted 2026 capex to
US$60–64B
([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/tsmc-posts-record-q2-profit-104626669.html),
[Investing.com](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-tsmc-lifts-2026-outlook-as-ai-demand-stays-hot-in-q2-2026-93CH-4794777)).

**Profitable?** Extremely. Q2 net income was NT$706.56B, **up 77.4%
year-on-year**, on a record 67.7% gross margin — for a manufacturer, that
margin is almost unheard of, and it reflects pricing power: customers have
nowhere else to go for leading-edge capacity.

**Main risk.** Geography. Most of TSMC's leading-edge capacity sits in
Taiwan, so the stock carries China–Taiwan geopolitical risk that no
financial metric captures. Secondary risks: its revenue is concentrated in
a few huge customers (Apple, Nvidia), and if AI capex ever slows, a company
spending $60B+ a year on fabs feels it fast. Established and very
profitable — but the tail risk is real and unhedgeable.

## 4. Concept of the day: the P/E ratio

**P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share.** It answers: *how many dollars
am I paying for each dollar of the company's annual profit?*

If a company earns $10 per share each year and the stock trades at $200,
the P/E is 20 — you're paying 20 years' worth of *current* profits. A high
P/E (say 50+) usually means the market expects profits to grow fast; a low
P/E (under ~12) often signals slow growth or trouble the market has already
spotted. Neither is automatically "good" — a cheap-looking P/E can be a
trap if earnings are about to fall, and an expensive one can be justified
if earnings double.

Watchlist relevance: yesterday's AMD move is a P/E story. When a stock
trades at a very high P/E, results that are merely "slightly better than
expected" can *disappoint* — the price already assumed brilliance. Two
cautions: P/E is meaningless for loss-makers (no E), and check whether the
"E" is trailing (last 12 months, real) or forward (next 12 months, a
forecast).

## 5. Hype check: AMD's +8% then −7% — "buy the rumor, sell the news"

Yesterday compressed a classic market lesson into 12 hours. AMD rose ~8%
*during* the session on rally excitement and earnings anticipation
([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/intel-soars-10-amd-jumps-163611976.html))
— then reported adjusted earnings *slightly ahead* of estimates and fell
~7% in premarket
([TheStreet](https://www.thestreet.com/stock-market-today/stock-market-today-dow-jones-sp-500-nasdaq-updates-aug-4-2026)).

The company didn't get worse overnight. What happened: by the time it
reported, the price had already absorbed extreme expectations, so "good"
wasn't good enough. The takeaway for a beginner: **a stock's reaction to
news measures the news against expectations, not against zero.** Buying
something *because* exciting news is coming is often buying at the moment
expectations (and price) peak. If the story is real, it will still be real
after the dust settles — and you'll pay a saner price for it.
